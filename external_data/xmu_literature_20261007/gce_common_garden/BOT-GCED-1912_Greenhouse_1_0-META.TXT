      2. Abstract: We examined trait differences and evolution across geographic clines among 
         continents of the intertidal grass Spartina alterniflora within its invasive and native 
         ranges. Between September and November 2014, we sampled vegetative and reproductive traits 
         in the field at 20 sites over 20° latitude in China (invasive range) and 28 sites over 17° 
         latitude in the US (native range). We grew both Chinese and US plants in a greenhouse 
         common garden for three years (2015 - 2017) to determine if differences in performance of 
         S. alterniflora between the introduced and native ranges were due to genetic differences 
         or differences in abiotic conditions.

         salinity and inundation, driven by climate change and anthropogenic factors, affect biotic

      5. Abstract: The Georgia Coastal Ecosystems (GCE) LTER is located along three adjacent sounds 
         on the Atlantic coast and includes both intertidal marshes and estuaries. Long-term 
         drivers of climate change, sea level rise and human alterations of the landscape will 
         cause transitions in dominant habitat types (state changes) within the GCE domain by 
         changing the amounts and patterns of water delivery across the landscape. These changes in 
         water delivery can be conceptualized as presses and pulses in river inflow, local runoff, 
         groundwater input, and tidal inundation, which will in turn manifest themselves as changes 
         in salinity and inundation patterns in the domain. The research proposed for GCE-III is 
         designed to address how variations in salinity and inundation, driven by climate change 
         and anthropogenic factors, affect biotic and ecosystem responses at different spatial and 
         temporal scales, and to predict the consequences of these changes for habitat provisioning 
         and carbon (C) sequestration across the coastal landscape. The goals are to: 1) Track 
         long-term changes in climate and human actions in the watershed and adjacent uplands, and 
         evaluate the effects of these drivers on domain boundary conditions. 2) Describe temporal 
         and spatial variability in physical, chemical, geological and biological, and to evaluate 
         how they are affected by variations in river inflow and other boundary conditions. 3) 
         Characterize the responses of three dominant habitats in the domain to pulses and presses 
         in salinity and inundation. 4) Describe patterns of habitat provisioning and C 
         sequestration and export in the GCE domain, and to evaluate how these might be affected by 
         changes in salinity and inundation. These efforts will be synthesized into a synoptic 
         understanding of both biotic and ecosystem responses to variations in salinity and 
         inundation driven by climate change and human activities, which will be used to assess 
         thresholds between habitats and the potential for state changes in the domain.

      2. Experimental or Sampling Design 
         a. Design Characteristics: 
            Study 1: Geographic study -- In China we sampled 10 locations ranging from 20.90 °N to 
               39.05 °N in September-November, 2014. To standardize phenology, we started at the 
               southernmost location (Leizhou) when seed set began in late September and ended at 
               the northernmost location (Tanggu) when seeds were being set in early November. In 
               the United States we sampled 14 locations ranging from 27.70 °N to 43.32 °N in 
               October and November 2014. Within each location, we worked at two sites, 2-3 km 
               apart, with a total of 20 sites in China, and 28 sites in the United States. In both 
               geographic ranges, S. alterniflora occupies lower elevations in the intertidal than 
               other plant species. As a practical matter, the easiest way to standardize elevation 
               across all sites was to focus on tall-form S. alterniflora, which we defined as the 
               tallest plants that were growing at each site. These plants were invariably growing 
               low in the marsh or along creek banks, and always occurred in monospecific stands.
            Study 2: Common Garden Study -- To determine if differences in performance of S. 
               alterniflora between the introduced and native ranges were due to genetic 
               differences or differences in abiotic conditions, we conducted a common-garden 
               experiment in a greenhouse at the Xiang’an campus of Xiamen University (24.62 °N, 
               118.31 °E). The greenhouse had a plastic roof to exclude rain and mesh sides to 
               exclude insects. The common garden consisted of ten rectangular plastic pools 
               (length: 1.2 m, width: 0.9 m, depth: 0.3 m). Each plastic pool contained 24 plastic 
               buckets (18 cm in diameter and 24 cm deep), which were grouped into four rows and 
               six columns. Each bucket was filled with mixture of 50% Jiffy’s peat substrate 
               (Jiffy Products International BV, Moerdijk, Netherlands) and 50% vermiculite (by 
               volume), and had four 1 cm diameter holes in the sides and one in the bottom to 
               allow exchange of water with the water in the pool. Pools were filled with 
               artificial sea water (10 PSU) to approx. 2 cm above the soil level in the pots. 
               Salinity was checked every other day and fresh water was added as needed to maintain 
               salinity. Water in the pools was completely replaced once a month. Fertilizer (C:N:P 
               15-15-15; 0.5 g per plant) was added to the pools in March every year (from 2015 
               -2017). In March 2015, seeds from each quadrat were germinated and grown in a growth 
               chamber until seedlings were approximately 5 cm tall. One haphazardly chosen 
               seedling per quadrat (100 from China; 140 from the United States) was transplanted 
               into a single, haphazardly selected plastic bucket, with each location (10 from 
               China; 14 from the United States) replicated once in each of the 10 pools.
         b. Permanent Plots:

         c. Data Collection Duration and Frequency: 
            Study 1: At each site, we sampled five 0.5 × 0.5 m quadrats in tall-form S. 
               alterniflora stands, with individual quadrats spaced greater than 30 meters apart. 
               In each quadrat, we measured the height of the three tallest S. alterniflora shoots 
               and counted the number of shoots that were greater than 25 cm tall to calculate 
               shoot density. We haphazardly collected 10 inflorescences within a meter of each 
               quadrat and counted the number of filled seeds in each inflorescence, distinguishing 
               filled from unfilled seeds. We calculated seed set (100*filled seeds/(total seeds)).
            Study 2: Using protocols developed in our earlier work (Liu et al., 2016, 2017), in 
               October 2015 we measured the height of the three tallest shoots in each pot when 
               most of plants had reached maturity in pots. If fewer than three shoots were 
               present, we measured all the shoots. We then counted the number of shoots greater 
               than 25 cm tall in each bucket. We collected inflorescences from the tallest three 
               flowering stems, and counted the number of filled seeds. If fewer than three 
               flowering shoots were present, we sampled all the shoots. In March 2016, we 
               separated a 15-20 cm long rhizome bearing one or two shoots from each clone, 
               transplanted the rhizome into a new bucket in a new soil mixture, and fertilized the 
               pool as above. This process was repeated in 2016-2017, to give three years of data. 
               Thus, data from each of the three years in the common garden were obtained from the 
               same clones that were propagated by rhizomes from one year to the next.

      3. Research Methods

               resulted in sampling 20 sites in China and 28 sites in the United States.
            Method 2: Geographic sampling -- We focused on tall-form Spartina Alterniflora, which 
               we defined as the tallest plants that were growing in each site. At each site, we 
               sampled five 0.5 × 0.5 m quadrats in tall-form S. alterniflora stands, with 
               individual quadrats spaced greater than 30 meters apart. In each quadrat, we 
               measured the height of the three tallest S. alterniflora shoots and counted the 
               number of shoots that were greater than 25 cm tall to calculate shoot density. We 
               haphazardly collected 10 inflorescences within a meter of each quadrat and counted 
               the number of filled seeds in each inflorescence, distinguishing filled from 
               unfilled seeds. We calculated both seed set (100*filled seeds/(total seeds).
            Method 3: Common garden design -- The common garden consisted of ten rectangular 
               plastic pools (length: 1.2 m, width: 0.9 m, depth: 0.3 m). Each plastic pool 
               contained 24 plastic buckets (18 cm in diameter and 24 cm deep), which were grouped 
               into four rows and six columns. Each bucket was filled with mixture of 50% Jiffy’s 
               peat substrate (Jiffy Products International BV, Moerdijk, Netherlands) and 50%
