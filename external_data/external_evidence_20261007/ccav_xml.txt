      <abstract><title>Abstract</title>

      <p id="d2e145">Coastal wetland vegetation plays a vital role in shoreline protection and ecosystem management, highlighting the need for accurate and high-resolution mapping of these unique and vulnerable habitats. Here, we present CCAV-10m, the first publicly available annual species-level wetland dataset for eastern coastal China at 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M1" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> resolution (2016–2023). This dataset was generated using a novel phenology-guided coastal wetland vegetation classification network (P_SVCN), which integrates Sentinel-1/2 satellite imagery with extensive in situ observations. Validation based on 4668 in situ samples confirms that P_SVCN delivers strong classification performance, achieving an overall accuracy of 0.916 and a Kappa coefficient of 0.898. Spatiotemporal analysis of CCAV-10m reveals that <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. is the dominant vegetation type, followed by <italic>Spartina alterniflora</italic>, whose coverage nearly equals the combined extent of <italic>Phragmites australis</italic>, mangroves, <italic>Scirpus mariqueter</italic>, and <italic>Tamarix chinensis</italic>. Notably, this work fills critical gaps in both spatial detail and temporal consistency across existing coastal wetland datasets, demonstrating the effectiveness of deep-learning-based fusion of optical and SAR data for high-resolution vegetation mapping. Regular updates to CCAV-10m will support long-term coastal wetland research, enhance invasive species monitoring, and inform wetland restoration and precision management efforts. The CCAV-10m dataset is openly accessible at  <ext-link xlink:href="https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.31077" ext-link-type="DOI">10.57760/sciencedb.31077</ext-link> <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx33" id="paren.1"/>.</p>

<sec id="Ch1.S1" sec-type="intro">
  <label>1</label><title>Introduction</title>
      <p id="d2e187">Coastal wetlands are complex ecosystems distributed along the land–sea transition zone, influenced jointly by tidal dynamics and salinity gradients, and mainly comprise salt marshes, mangroves, tidal flats, and brackish wetland forests <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx12 bib1.bibx4 bib1.bibx44" id="paren.2"/>. Tropical mangroves cover approximately 150 000 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M2" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:msup><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">km</mml:mi><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">2</mml:mn></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, while temperate tidal marshes exceed 45 000 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M3" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:msup><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">km</mml:mi><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">2</mml:mn></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx66 bib1.bibx36" id="paren.3"/>, together forming one of the most productive and carbon-rich ecosystems on earth <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx37 bib1.bibx5" id="paren.4"/>. Coastal wetlands function not only as geochemical “sinks” but also as highly effective “green filters” that intercept and remove land-derived pollutants <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx58 bib1.bibx78" id="paren.5"/>. Their ecosystem service value can reach up to USD 194 000 per <inline-formula><mml:math id="M4" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> per year, encompassing carbon sequestration and greenhouse gas regulation, coastal protection, fisheries support, water purification, and biodiversity maintenance <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx17 bib1.bibx50 bib1.bibx11 bib1.bibx13 bib1.bibx1" id="paren.6"/>.</p>
      <p id="d2e236">China's coastal wetlands represent a key component of its national blue carbon ecosystems, spanning tropical, subtropical, and temperate climate zones <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx6 bib1.bibx58 bib1.bibx64" id="paren.7"/>. Their broad distribution and substantial carbon sequestration capacity place them at the core of China's blue carbon strategy <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx22" id="paren.8"/>. Dominant vegetation types in Chinese coastal wetlands include <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>P. australis</italic>, <italic>T. chinensis</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, and mangroves <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx21" id="paren.9"/>. Among these, <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, introduced in the 1990s, has rapidly expanded across Jiangsu, Shanghai, Zhejiang, and Fujian, with scattered occurrences in Liaoning, Tianjin, Hebei, and Shandong <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx41 bib1.bibx7" id="paren.10"/>. In many East Asian countries, biological invasions, tidal flat reclamation, and wetland restoration occur simultaneously; thus, timely and fine-scale species-level monitoring is crucial for tracking invasion dynamics, evaluating restoration outcomes, and supporting biodiversity conservation and sustainable coastal management <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx60 bib1.bibx56" id="paren.11"/>.</p>
      <p id="d2e273">Coastal wetlands are composed of herbaceous plants, grasses, and low-stature shrubs that are adapted to regular or occasional tidal inundation <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx28" id="paren.12"/>. Due to the inherent complexity of vegetation, tidal dynamics, and anthropogenic disturbances, mapping of coastal wetlands remains challenging <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx80" id="paren.13"/>. To date, a variety of remote sensing data sources have been widely applied to monitor coastal wetland vegetation. In multispectral optical imagery, medium- to low-resolution datasets such as MODIS and AVHRR offer high temporal resolution <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx18 bib1.bibx77" id="paren.14"/>, which makes them suitable for wetland information extraction; however, their spatial resolution is insufficient for vegetation species-level classification <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx59 bib1.bibx73" id="paren.15"/>. High-resolution optical imagery also plays an important role in wetland vegetation classification, but it is limited by cloud cover and atmospheric effects <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx25 bib1.bibx47" id="paren.16"/>. Moreover, traditional remote sensing methods based on vegetation indices and phenological features are still widely applied. For example, the enhanced phenology-based vegetation index algorithm proposed by <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx72" id="text.17"/> achieved an overall accuracy of 86.67 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M5" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> for coastal salt marshes along the Bohai Sea, while <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx56" id="text.18"/> applied a time-series model and phenological parameters to classify Jiangsu coastal salt marshes with an average overall accuracy of 84.8 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M6" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> (<inline-formula><mml:math id="M7" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mo>±</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">5.5</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> <inline-formula><mml:math id="M8" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>). Although these approaches are effective for short-term monitoring, they often struggle to capture complex spatial structures and inter-annual dynamics fully.</p>
      <p id="d2e332">With the advancement of remote sensing technology, research on wetland vegetation classification has gradually shifted from traditional optical image-based methods to multi-source data fusion techniques. Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), with its all-weather and all-time imaging capabilities, has become a key complement to optical imagery <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx61 bib1.bibx62" id="paren.19"/>. In recent years, researchers have attempted to integrate SAR and optical imagery to improve the accuracy of salt marsh vegetation classification. For instance, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx69" id="text.20"/> integrated Sentinel-1 SAR and Sentinel-2 optical imagery to classify wetland vegetation in the Yellow River Delta, China, achieving an overall accuracy of 93.51 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M9" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and a Kappa coefficient of 0.917. Similarly, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx52" id="text.21"/> combined Sentinel-1 SAR and Sentinel-2 for multi-level wetland classification in the St. Lucia Wetlands, South Africa, with an overall accuracy of 90.7 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M10" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. At the same time, with the development of deep learning, convolutional neural networks and attention mechanisms have been increasingly applied to wetland vegetation classification, significantly improving spatial detail representation and cross-temporal feature extraction. For example, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx54" id="text.22"/> employed a hybrid 2D–3D CNN to achieve an annual average classification accuracy exceeding 97 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M11" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> for Jiangsu coastal wetlands, and <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx29" id="text.23"/> used a U-Net model to classify wetlands in South Carolina, USA, achieving a maximum accuracy of 90 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M12" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. Although these regional studies have achieved promising results, they mostly focus on single-date or short-term analyses. Therefore, long-term, species-level wetland monitoring at a national scale remains limited, which is crucial for coastal ecosystem management and wetland conservation.</p>
      <p id="d2e384">In this study, we developed a novel phenology-guided coastal wetland vegetation classification network integrating Sentinel-1/2 (P_SVCN) based on deep learning and employed it to generate the CCAV-10m dataset (<ext-link xlink:href="https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.31077" ext-link-type="DOI">10.57760/sciencedb.31077</ext-link>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx33" id="altparen.24"/>), an annual spatiotemporal China's coastal wetland vegetation dataset, 2016–2023. The P_SVCN model integrates Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 imagery with in situ data, maintaining the sensitivity of SAR data to vegetation structure and moisture while adaptively incorporating the spectral and phenological information from optical imagery. P_SVCN significantly improves classification accuracy, enabling species-level mapping of coastal wetland vegetation. CCAV-10m provides a valuable spatiotemporal resource for long-term monitoring, ecological research, and sustainable management of China's coastal wetlands.</p>
</sec>
<sec id="Ch1.S2">
  <label>2</label><title>Study area</title>
      <p id="d2e401">The Chinese coastline extends approximately 32 000 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M13" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">km</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, including 14 000 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M14" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">km</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> of island coastlines, ranging from the Yalu River estuary in northern Liaoning Province to the Beilun River estuary in southern Guangxi and Hainan Island <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx21 bib1.bibx22" id="paren.25"/>. This study focuses on eight coastal provinces and municipalities: Liaoning, Hebei, Tianjin, Shandong, Jiangsu, Shanghai, Zhejiang, and Fujian, spanning 23<inline-formula><mml:math id="M15" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">°</mml:mi><mml:msup><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">30</mml:mn><mml:mo>′</mml:mo></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>–40<inline-formula><mml:math id="M16" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">°</mml:mi><mml:msup><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">03</mml:mn><mml:mo>′</mml:mo></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> N latitude and 115<inline-formula><mml:math id="M17" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">°</mml:mi><mml:msup><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">50</mml:mn><mml:mo>′</mml:mo></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>–124<inline-formula><mml:math id="M18" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">°</mml:mi><mml:msup><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">22</mml:mn><mml:mo>′</mml:mo></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> E longitude (Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F1"/>). The study area covers both temperate and subtropical climatic zones, supporting a diverse range of wetland vegetation types <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx23" id="paren.26"/>.  Wetland boundaries were delineated using the GLC_FCS30D dataset <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx79" id="paren.27"/>, with a 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M19" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">km</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> buffer inland and seaward from the coastline applied for analysis. Common wetland species include <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>P. australis</italic>, <italic>T. chinensis</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, and mangroves <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx8" id="paren.28"/>. <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, native to the Americas, was introduced to China in the 1990s and is now widespread in Jiangsu, Shanghai, Zhejiang, and Fujian, with scattered occurrences in Liaoning, Tianjin, Hebei, and Shandong <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx41 bib1.bibx7" id="paren.29"/>. <italic>S. mariqueter</italic> occurs mainly in Shanghai, Jiangsu, and the Hangzhou Bay area <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx54 bib1.bibx82" id="paren.30"/>, while <italic>T. chinensis</italic> and <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. are concentrated in northern provinces <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx55" id="paren.31"/>, and mangroves are restricted to the southern coast, reaching their northern limit in Yueqing, Zhejiang <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx67" id="paren.32"/>.</p>

<sec id="Ch1.S3">
  <label>3</label><title>Data and methods</title>
<sec id="Ch1.S3.SS1">
  <label>3.1</label><title>Sentinel-1</title>
      <p id="d2e563">We used multisource remote sensing data, including Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 imagery, to improve coastal vegetation classification <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx81" id="paren.33"/>. Sentinel-1 Level-1 Ground Range Detected (GRD) data <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx48" id="paren.34"/> at 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M20" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> resolution were preprocessed in SNAP following orbital correction, thermal noise removal, radiometric calibration, speckle filtering, and terrain correction, with reflectivity converted from linear scale to decibels <inline-formula><mml:math id="M21" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mo>(</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">dB</mml:mi><mml:mo>)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx32" id="paren.35"/>. A total of 8018 scenes spanning January 2016 to December 2023 in eastern China were composited annually using a mean function. These composites captured regional climatic patterns while reducing tidal and seasonal variability in salt marsh vegetation.</p>
</sec>
<sec id="Ch1.S3.SS2">
  <label>3.2</label><title>Sentinel-2</title>
      <p id="d2e603">Sentinel-2 imagery was selected according to the key phenological stages of coastal vegetation, namely green and senescence <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx80" id="paren.36"/>. To ensure high spectral fidelity, a stringent cloud-cover threshold of less than 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M22" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> was applied during the scene selection process. Across the study period, 320 high-quality scenes of dual-temporal optical imagery were processed in SNAP and ENVI, including resampling and band fusion <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx65" id="paren.37"/>. Distinct from the year-round continuous observation strategy employed for Sentinel-1 SAR data, this targeted sampling of dual-phase optical imagery aims to capture the maximum inter-species spectral contrast while minimizing atmospheric interference.</p>
      <p id="d2e620">For each phenophase, four spectral bands with the highest vegetation contrast – B02 (blue), B03 (green), B04 (red), and B08 (near-infrared) – were extracted, yielding eight optical channels <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx3" id="paren.38"/>. Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) maps were derived from the red and near-infrared bands of both phenophases to capture differences in vegetation status. All SAR and optical images were co-registered and resampled to a consistent 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M23" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> resolution to ensure data alignment and comparability.</p>
</sec>
<sec id="Ch1.S3.SS3">
  <label>3.3</label><title>In situ data</title>
      <p id="d2e643">We conducted field surveys and vegetation sampling across wetlands along the coast of eastern China, between 2017–2018. High-precision GPS (Trimble Juno 3D) and a DJI Phantom-4 RTK unmanned aerial vehicle were combined to systematically record site information. Following a stratified sampling design, plots were established for six representative salt marsh vegetation types: <italic>P. australis</italic>, <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., <italic>T. chinensis</italic>, mangroves, and <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>. Each plot measured <inline-formula><mml:math id="M24" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">10</mml:mn><mml:mspace linebreak="nobreak" width="0.125em"/><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mo>×</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">10</mml:mn><mml:mspace linebreak="nobreak" width="0.125em"/><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, with a minimum distance of 100 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M25" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> between adjacent plots to ensure spatial independence and representativeness. Within each plot, three <inline-formula><mml:math id="M26" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">0.5</mml:mn><mml:mspace linebreak="nobreak" width="0.125em"/><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mo>×</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">0.5</mml:mn><mml:mspace linebreak="nobreak" width="0.125em"/><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> quadrats were randomly located. GPS coordinates of each quadrat were recorded with <inline-formula><mml:math id="M27" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mo>±</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">5</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> <inline-formula><mml:math id="M28" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> accuracy to guarantee even spatial coverage across vegetation types. Plot locations were further verified and corrected using high-resolution imagery from Google Earth to ensure consistency with actual vegetation distribution. To complement the field data, we also compiled additional wetland vegetation validation points from the literature <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx80 bib1.bibx30 bib1.bibx57" id="paren.39"/>.</p>
      <p id="d2e731">In total, 2665 ground truth points were collected (Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F1"/>), comprising 948 <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, 678 <italic>P. australis</italic>, 432 <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., 226 <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, 263 mangrove, and 118 <italic>T. chinensis</italic> points. Experienced researchers then conducted visual interpretation and manual labeling of additional points using Google Earth imagery, resulting in 2072 <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, 2435 <italic>P. australis</italic>, 2632 <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., 2901 <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, 1803 mangrove, and 1050 <italic>T. chinensis</italic> points. Collected samples underwent systematic quality control, producing a reliable dataset for training and validating remote sensing classification models.</p>
</sec>
<sec id="Ch1.S3.SS4">
  <label>3.4</label><title>A phenology-guided coastal wetland vegetation classification network integrating Sentinel-1/2 (P_SVCN)</title>
      <p id="d2e776">In this study, we developed a phenology-guided coastal wetland vegetation classification network integrating Sentinel-1/2 (P_SVCN) with a dual-branch multi-source attention mechanism for the fine-scale classification of coastal wetland vegetation along the Chinese coast. The training dataset was based on Sentinel-1 imagery from 2017–2018, including VV and VH polarizations, along with three derived SAR features (SAR-Diff, SAR-NDVI, and SAR-SUM). Simultaneously, dual-phase Sentinel-2 optical imagery was incorporated, extracting eight spectral bands (B02, B03, B04, and B08 at two phenological phases) and their corresponding NDVI indices as input features. Vegetation types recorded at the sampled locations corresponding to the imagery periods were used as ground truth for supervised training. The dataset was strictly split into training (70 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M29" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, 10 890 samples) and validation (30 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M30" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, 4668 samples) subsets. The trained model was then applied to classify coastal wetland vegetation from 2016 to 2023 along the Chinese coast.</p>
<sec id="Ch1.S3.SS4.SSS1">
  <label>3.4.1</label><title>SAR feature construction from Sentinel-1</title>
      <p id="d2e803">Sentinel-1 SAR imagery offers significant advantages for monitoring wetland vegetation, being highly sensitive to key variables such as total vegetation water content, canopy structure dynamics, and vegetation cover <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx63 bib1.bibx75" id="paren.40"/>. With its all-weather, all-time radar observation capability, Sentinel-1 can reliably acquire data under diverse meteorological conditions, providing robust support for temporal analyses of vegetation dynamics <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx51 bib1.bibx42" id="paren.41"/>. The C-band radar signals can penetrate the canopy and effectively capture interactions between vegetation and soil <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx40" id="paren.42"/>, making Sentinel-1 particularly suitable for long-term monitoring and dynamic studies of wetland ecosystems <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx70 bib1.bibx74" id="paren.43"/>.</p>
      <p id="d2e818">Backscatter coefficients in VV and VH polarization provide insight into vegetation–ground interactions <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx63 bib1.bibx39 bib1.bibx46" id="paren.44"/>. Vegetated areas typically exhibit higher surface roughness than non-vegetated regions, resulting in distinct backscatter differences, although VH polarization signals may be influenced by double-bounce effects from soil surfaces <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx62 bib1.bibx15" id="paren.45"/>. To suppress the inherent speckle noise in SAR imagery and capture stable annual structural signatures of coastal vegetation, we utilized all available Sentinel-1 scenes acquired throughout the entire study year. An annual mean compositing strategy was applied to generate a representative backscatter coefficient for each pixel. Based on these considerations, this study utilized the processed VV and VH single-polarization SAR data along with three derived indices, including SAR_NDVI, SAR_SUM, and SAR_Diff, for the classification of wetland vegetation types along eastern China's coastal zones.</p>
      <p id="d2e827">SAR_NDVI quantifies normalized differences in backscatter coefficients between VV and VH polarization states, providing a reliable basis for distinguishing vegetation types <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx62" id="paren.46"/>. SAR_Diff effectively suppresses double-bounce effects from straw or surface structures, enhancing the ability to detect subtle differences between vegetation and soil, thereby improving classification accuracy <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx38" id="paren.47"/>. SAR_SUM integrates the total backscatter intensity from both VV and VH channels, offering a comprehensive representation of canopy and surface structure information <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx32" id="paren.48"/>.</p>
</sec>
<sec id="Ch1.S3.SS4.SSS2">
  <label>3.4.2</label><title>Phenology-based spectral feature extraction from Sentinel-2</title>
      <p id="d2e847">The dominant vegetation types in different climatic zones exhibit distinct phenological rhythms <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx76" id="paren.49"/>. In temperate regions, vegetation growth is constrained by low temperatures and dormancy periods, resulting in a relatively concentrated growing season <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx35" id="paren.50"/>, whereas in subtropical regions, the growing period is prolonged and phenological transitions are relatively gradual. Using imagery acquired at a uniform time across the entire coastal zone would lead to phenological mismatches among regions, thereby increasing spectral confusion and classification errors <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx72" id="paren.51"/>. Therefore, this study adopted a climate-zone-based strategy, selecting paired images corresponding to the green and senescence stages within each zone to enhance the spectral separability among vegetation types. To fully leverage the spectral differences of coastal wetland vegetation across phenological stages <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx20 bib1.bibx16" id="paren.52"/>, dual-phase optical imagery was selected based on the growth patterns of representative vegetation within each climatic zone.</p>
      <p id="d2e862">The annual NDVI time series for the dominant coastal vegetation species across eastern China's coastal zones. (Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F2"/>) clearly reveal distinct seasonal dynamics for each species.  The temperate zone was defined as the area north of the Qinling–Huaihe line (approximately 33° N), where the mean January temperature is below 0 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M31" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">°</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">C</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx2 bib1.bibx9" id="paren.53"/>, primarily including the coastal provinces of Liaoning, Tianjin, Hebei, Shandong, and northern Jiangsu. The North Subtropical zone extends from the Qinling–Huaihe line southward to the northernmost mangrove distribution in Yueqing, Zhejiang (<inline-formula><mml:math id="M32" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mo>≈</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">28</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>°21<sup>′</sup> N) <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx71" id="paren.54"/>, covering southern Jiangsu, Shanghai, and northern Zhejiang. The Subtropical zone lies south of this boundary, encompassing southern Zhejiang and Fujian provinces. For the temperate, North Subtropical, and Subtropical regions, two key time points (<inline-formula><mml:math id="M34" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>t</mml:mi><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">1</mml:mn></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and <inline-formula><mml:math id="M35" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>t</mml:mi><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">2</mml:mn></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) corresponding to the peak growth and senescence stages were identified. Within each climatic zone, the dual-phase selection was kept consistent to ensure uniform input features. For spectral feature construction, four key optical bands – blue (B2), green (B3), red (B4), and near-infrared (B8) – were extracted for each pixel at both phenological stages, and the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) was calculated from the red and near-infrared bands (Eq. <xref ref-type="disp-formula" rid="Ch1.E1"/>). The dual-phase optical bands and corresponding NDVI values were then combined into a spectral feature vector (Eq. <xref ref-type="disp-formula" rid="Ch1.E2"/>).

<sec id="Ch1.S3.SS4.SSS4">
  <label>3.4.4</label><title>Model Training and Implementation Details</title>
      <p id="d2e1830">The proposed P_SVCN framework was implemented using the PyTorch deep learning library and trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. To optimize the network parameters for the classification of six coastal wetland vegetation types, we employed the Adam optimizer <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx27" id="paren.57"/> with a weight decay of <inline-formula><mml:math id="M55" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">1</mml:mn><mml:mo>×</mml:mo><mml:msup><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">10</mml:mn><mml:mrow><mml:mo>-</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">5</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> to provide regularization and enhance generalization. The initial learning rate was set to <inline-formula><mml:math id="M56" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">1</mml:mn><mml:mo>×</mml:mo><mml:msup><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">10</mml:mn><mml:mrow><mml:mo>-</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">4</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, and the epsilon parameter (<inline-formula><mml:math id="M57" display="inline"><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">ϵ</mml:mi></mml:math></inline-formula>) for numerical stability was maintained at <inline-formula><mml:math id="M58" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">1</mml:mn><mml:mo>×</mml:mo><mml:msup><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">10</mml:mn><mml:mrow><mml:mo>-</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">8</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>.</p>
      <p id="d2e1897">To ensure stable convergence and prevent the model from becoming trapped in local optima during the fine-grained classification of multi-source Sentinel-1/2 data, a MultiStepLR scheduler was utilized. The learning rate was decayed by a factor of 0.1 (<inline-formula><mml:math id="M59" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">γ</mml:mi><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">0.1</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) at specified milestones, specifically at epochs 30, 60, and 90. The Cross-Entropy loss function was adopted as the objective function to minimize categorical errors across the six classes. The entire training process spanned 100 epochs with a mini-batch size of 32.</p>

<sec id="Ch1.S4.SS1">
  <label>4.1</label><title>CCAV-10m dataset</title>
      <p id="d2e2334">We present CCAV-10m, an annual 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M62" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> resolution vegetation dataset that accurately captures the spatiotemporal dynamics of coastal wetland species across eastern China from 2016 to 2023. The dataset has a spatial resolution of 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M63" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and represents the first publicly released annual time series of coastal wetland vegetation in eastern China. CCAV-10m distinguishes six representative coastal wetland vegetation types–<italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>P. australis</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, mangroves, and <italic>T. chinensis</italic> – achieving species-level classification. Model validation shows a high overall accuracy (OA) of 0.916 and a Kappa coefficient of 0.898, indicating stable and reliable identification across all vegetation types.</p>
      <p id="d2e2369">In 2023, coastal wetlands in eastern China covered a total area of 617 976.38 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M64" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, comprising six dominant vegetation types: <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>P. australis</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, <italic>T. chinensis</italic>, and mangroves (Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F4"/>). From 2016 to 2023, coastal wetland vegetation exhibited pronounced interannual dynamics across eastern China (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T2"/>), with <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. as the dominant type, followed by <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, whose coverage is nearly equivalent to the combined extent of <italic>P. australis</italic>, mangroves, <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, and <italic>T. chinensis</italic>. <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, as an invasive saltmarsh species,  maintained a relatively stable area, fluctuating between 20 202 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M65" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and 25 918 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M66" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, with occasional declines. <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. showed a notable increasing trend, expanding from 24 436 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M67" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> in 2018 to 35 452 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M68" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> in 2023. <italic>P. australis</italic> exhibited considerable interannual variability, reaching its maximum of 22 893 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M69" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> in 2020, then decreasing to 10 614 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M70" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> in 2023. <italic>T. chinensis</italic> and <italic>S. mariqueter</italic> occupied relatively smaller areas, although <italic>T. chinensis</italic> experienced localized expansion in 2020 and 2022. As a key component of coastal protection, mangroves increased from 4894 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M71" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> in 2017 to 7648 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M72" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> in 2023, showing an overall upward trend.</p>

<sec id="Ch1.S4.SS2">
  <label>4.2</label><title>Performance evaluation of P_SVCN</title>
      <p id="d2e3100">A total of 15 558 in-situ data points were collected across eastern China's coastal wetlands and partitioned into training (70 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M94" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) and validation (30 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M95" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, <inline-formula><mml:math id="M96" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>n</mml:mi><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">4668</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) sets following a stratified random sampling principle based on both vegetation types and geographic regions. To further evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed P_SVCN model, we compared its performance with the Salt marsh Vegetation Classification Network (SVCN) <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx32" id="paren.60"/> on the same validation dataset. As shown in Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T3"/>, P_SVCN outperformed SVCN across all vegetation classes. For <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, P_SVCN achieved a producer's accuracy (PA) of 0.927, user's accuracy (UA) of 0.882, and F1 score of 0.904, compared to 0.881, 0.820, and 0.849 for SVCN. For <italic>P. australis</italic>, the corresponding values were 0.921, 0.880, and 0.900 for P_SVCN vs. 0.876, 0.829, and 0.852 for SVCN. For <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., P_SVCN yielded a PA of 0.904, UA of 0.954, and F1 score of 0.928, higher than SVCN (<inline-formula><mml:math id="M97" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mtext>PA</mml:mtext><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">0.851</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, <inline-formula><mml:math id="M98" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mtext>UA</mml:mtext><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">0.920</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, <inline-formula><mml:math id="M99" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mtext>F1</mml:mtext><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">0.884</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>). Similarly, <italic>S. mariqueter</italic> (P_SVCN: 0.937/0.953/0.945; SVCN: 0.902/0.927/0.914), mangroves (P_SVCN: 0.918/0.979/0.948; SVCN: 0.890/0.953/0.921), and <italic>T. chinensis</italic> (P_SVCN: 0.846/0.813/0.829; SVCN: 0.811/0.796/0.803) also exhibited higher accuracy metrics under P_SVCN. The overall accuracy (OA) and Kappa coefficient for P_SVCN were 0.916 and 0.898, respectively, exceeding those of SVCN (<inline-formula><mml:math id="M100" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mtext>OA</mml:mtext><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">0.874</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, <inline-formula><mml:math id="M101" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:mtext>Kappa</mml:mtext><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">0.845</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>). These results indicate that the dual-branch multi-source attention design of P_SVCN effectively enhances classification performance for coastal wetland vegetation types.</p>

      <p id="d2e3524">This marked performance leap is fundamentally attributed to the synergistic integration of multi-temporal optical phenology and Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) structural attributes within the P_SVCN architecture. In contrast to the SVCN model, which relies exclusively on Sentinel-1 SAR backscattering coefficients (<inline-formula><mml:math id="M107" display="inline"><mml:mrow><mml:msup><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">σ</mml:mi><mml:mn mathvariant="normal">0</mml:mn></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>), P_SVCN introduces multi-temporal optical observations to construct a high-dimensional Phenological-Spectral Vector (PSV).</p>
      <p id="d2e3538">Within complex coastal wetland ecotones, discriminating between vegetation species with analogous physical architectures is inherently constrained when relying solely on SAR observations. Furthermore, radar backscatter is highly susceptible to fluctuations in the surface dielectric constant induced by periodic tidal inundation. P_SVCN effectively circumvents the information bottleneck of single-source radar data by utilizing the PSV to characterize species-specific growth trajectories – most notably the distinctive “red beach” spectral signature of <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. during the senescence stage (Phase 2). Simultaneously, the framework preserves the deterministic advantages of SAR in characterizing canopy volume and biomass. By leveraging a multi-source attention mechanism, P_SVCN facilitates dynamic complementarity between the optical “biological fingerprint” and the radar-derived “physical structure,” thereby substantially mitigating classification uncertainty.</p>

  <label>5</label><title>Discussion</title>
<sec id="Ch1.S5.SS1">
  <label>5.1</label><title>The CCAV-10m dataset: Filling a critical gap in coastal wetland vegetation mapping</title>
      <p id="d2e3561">We introduce CCAV-10m, an annual 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M108" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> coastal wetland vegetation dataset generated using the P-SVCN model, which captures the spatial and temporal dynamics of eastern coastal China's wetland vegetation from 2016 to 2023. As the first publicly available species-level coastal wetland dataset for this region, CCAV-10m provides fine-resolution mapping of six representative vegetation types–<italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>P. australis</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, mangroves, and <italic>T. chinensis</italic>. Model evaluation demonstrates robust performance, with an overall accuracy of 0.916 and a Kappa coefficient of 0.898.</p>
      <p id="d2e3588">Compared with existing coastal wetland datasets (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T4"/>), CCAV-10m demonstrates significant advantages in terms of spatial coverage, functional composition, and temporal continuity. First, regarding spatial coverage, CCAV-10m spans the entire coastal zone of eastern China, and its 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M109" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> resolution enables precise delineation of complex intertidal vegetation mosaics. In contrast, SaltMarshVegYRD <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx74" id="paren.62"/> is limited to the Yellow River Delta with a 30 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M110" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> resolution, and CMSA <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx31" id="paren.63"/>, although also at 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M111" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> resolution, focuses solely on a single invasive species, failing to systematically represent coastal vegetation patterns at the national scale. Second, in terms of functional composition, CCAV-10m provides species-level refinement, differentiating six representative vegetation types along the Chinese coast: <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>P. australis</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, mangroves, and <italic>T. chinensis</italic>. By comparison, CCSV <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx80" id="paren.64"/> classifies only broad salt marsh categories, and CMSA focuses exclusively on the invasive species <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, both lacking intra-community functional differentiation. Finally, in the temporal dimension, CCAV-10m offers a continuous annual time series from 2016 to 2023, enabling long-term and systematic monitoring of coastal wetland dynamics. In contrast, CCSV contains only a single epoch in 2020, and CMSA provides annual sequences but only for a single species.</p>

  <label>5.2</label><title>Provincial-scale dynamics of eastern China's coastal wetland vegetation (2016–2023)</title>
      <p id="d2e3847">Coastal wetland vegetation exhibited clear provincial-scale differences in eastern China from 2016 to 2023. In Liaoning Province, the dominant vegetation types included <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., and <italic>P. australis</italic>. Hebei, Shandong, and Tianjin showed similar compositions, characterized mainly by <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., and <italic>P. australis</italic>, with additional occurrences of <italic>T. chinensis</italic> in specific areas. In Jiangsu Province, coastal wetlands were primarily dominated by <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., and <italic>P. australis</italic>. Shanghai featured a vegetation assemblage composed of <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, and <italic>P. australis</italic>. In Zhejiang Province, <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> and <italic>S. mariqueter</italic> were widely distributed, accompanied by patches of mangroves and <italic>P. australis</italic>. Fujian Province, located at the southernmost part of the study area, was characterized by extensive mangrove ecosystems, with additional distributions of <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> and <italic>P. australis</italic>.</p>
      <p id="d2e3907">Coastal wetlands showed clear provincial differences in area and species composition(Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F7"/>). Jiangsu, Shandong, and Zhejiang had the largest wetland areas, followed by Fujian, Liaoning, and Shanghai, while Hebei and Tianjin were smaller. <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> and <italic>P. australis</italic> dominated most provinces, with <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., <italic>T. chinensis</italic>, <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, and mangroves restricted but ecologically important. In Liaoning, wetlands were stable, dominated by <italic>P. australis</italic> (450–530 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M116" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) and <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> (450–9030 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M117" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>). Tianjin had scattered wetlands (400–780 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M118" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) with alternating dominance of <italic>P. australis</italic> and <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp.. Hebei showed pronounced species dynamics, with <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> stable (1320–2420 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M119" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) and <italic>P. australis</italic> increasing from 2870 to 3730 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M120" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. Shandong experienced significant changes, <italic>P. australis</italic> rising from 3910 to 7120 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M121" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, reflecting Spartina control effects. Jiangsu, the largest coastal wetland province, had over 60 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M122" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> combined <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> and <italic>P. australis</italic>, with <italic>P. australis</italic> slightly increasing from 3740 to 4710 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M123" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. In Shanghai, wetlands shrank from 1300 to 970 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M124" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, while <italic>S. mariqueter</italic> partially recovered post-2021. Zhejiang's wetlands alternated between <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> and <italic>P. australis</italic> (2990–5240 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M125" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>), and <italic>S. mariqueter</italic> remained stable (2110–4210 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M126" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>). Fujian's wetlands were relatively stable (2290–3850 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M127" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">ha</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>), dominated by <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>. Detailed annual values for each province and species are provided in the Appendix A (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="TA1"/>). Coastal wetlands exhibited clear provincial differences, with <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> and <italic>P. australis</italic> dominating most regions, while <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., <italic>T. chinensis</italic>, <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, and mangroves were more restricted in distribution yet remained ecologically significant, reflecting localized dynamic adjustments within a generally stable vegetation structure.</p>

      <p id="d2e4446">From the classification results, the S1-only configuration slightly outperformed S2 in most vegetation types, particularly in the discrimination of <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> and <italic>P. australis</italic>. Sentinel-1 C-band SAR captures canopy structure, surface roughness, and moisture scattering features <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx75 bib1.bibx42 bib1.bibx49" id="paren.68"/>, which are less sensitive to optical disturbances caused by turbid water and cloudy conditions, thus providing advantages in identifying structurally distinct vegetation. In contrast, the S2-only configuration, although leveraging dual-temporal imagery to enhance spectral temporal information, is affected by surface albedo variations and spectral mixing in intertidal zones <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx14" id="paren.69"/>, resulting in misclassifications between spectrally similar types such as <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. and <italic>T. chinensis</italic>. The S1<inline-formula><mml:math id="M137" display="inline"><mml:mo>+</mml:mo></mml:math></inline-formula>S2 fusion significantly improved per-class producer's accuracy (PA) and user's accuracy (UA), effectively mitigating the limitations of single data sources. The structural and moisture information from SAR complements the spectral and vegetation index features from optical imagery, enabling stable model performance across different tidal stages and climatic conditions, particularly enhancing the separability of <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. and <italic>T. chinensis</italic>.</p>
      <p id="d2e4482">Nevertheless, residual uncertainties persist. First, <italic>T. chinensis</italic> and <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. are often interspersed in the upper wetland, with similar temporal and phenological characteristics <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx19 bib1.bibx24 bib1.bibx68" id="paren.70"/>, making complete discrimination challenging even under multi-source fusion. Second, <italic>S. mariqueter</italic> has a narrow and highly patchy distribution <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx21" id="paren.71"/>, which may result in omission errors under 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M138" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> resolution. Third, despite the overall robustness of the model, localized “out-of-range” misclassifications were observed (e.g. mangrove patches erroneously identified in Hangzhou Bay, exceeding their northern distribution limit).</p>
      <p id="d2e4509">Future studies could integrate higher-resolution SAR data (e.g. TerraSAR-X, GF-3) and incorporate geographic prior knowledge – such as latitudinal distribution limits and climatic thresholds – as spatial constraints in the post-processing phase. Such a knowledge-guided approach will effectively filter out biologically inconsistent errors and further enhance the spatial reliability of coastal wetland mapping.</p>
</sec>
<sec id="Ch1.S5.SS4">
  <label>5.4</label><title>Limitations of the CCAV-10m dataset</title>
      <p id="d2e4520">While the CCAV-10m dataset demonstrates high overall thematic fidelity, localized inter-annual fluctuations in species distribution (as illustrated in Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F7"/>) necessitate a rigorous interpretation within the context of complex environmental background fields. The spatial distribution and spectral identification of coastal wetland vegetation are profoundly influenced by tidal regimes, salinity gradients, and phenological dynamics <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx45" id="paren.72"/>. Tidal inundation typically attenuates reflectance in the near-infrared (NIR) spectrum, frequently leading to the omission of low-stature vegetation due to water-induced signal absorption <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx26" id="paren.73"/>. Simultaneously, spatial variations in salinity gradients can alter the biophysical parameters of <italic>P. australis</italic>,  inducing localized spectral deviations that exacerbate the risk of confusion with invasive species such as <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx83 bib1.bibx10" id="paren.74"/>.</p>

      <fig id="F8" specific-use="star"><label>Figure 8</label><caption><p id="d2e4543">Comparative analysis of environmental constraints in the Liaoning coastal zone (2022–2023). <bold>(a)</bold> Atmospheric Constraints: Mean cloud cover <inline-formula><mml:math id="M139" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mo>(</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi><mml:mo>)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> during the green (Phase 1) and senescence (Phase 2) stages. <bold>(b)</bold> Hydrologic Signal: Mean Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) during Phase 2 (senescence).</p></caption>
          <graphic xlink:href="https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/2907/2026/essd-18-2907-2026-f08.png"/>

        </fig>

      <p id="d2e4570">A prominent case study is the observed abrupt fluctuations of <italic>P. australis</italic> and <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. along the Liaoning coast between 2022–2023. Meteorological back-analysis reveals that these shifts are not indicative of actual ecological succession but are instead “environmental artifacts” induced by extreme hydrometeorological anomalies. From August to September 2023, the Liaoning coastal zone was struck by the consecutive impacts of Typhoons Doksuri and Khanun, which triggered rare and catastrophic precipitation events <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx34" id="paren.75"/>.</p>
      <p id="d2e4583">This extreme climate directly compromised the classification logic through two primary mechanisms.First, the physical submergence and subsequent spectral masking. Since <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. is a low-stature species with a typical height of less than 30 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M140" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">cm</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, it is highly susceptible to complete submergence by typhoon-induced storm surges and inland flooding. During the critical “red beach” phenological window in 2023, record-breaking rainfall caused widespread surface water pooling. The regional mean NDWI increased from <inline-formula><mml:math id="M141" display="inline"><mml:mo>-</mml:mo></mml:math></inline-formula>0.041 in 2022 to <inline-formula><mml:math id="M142" display="inline"><mml:mo>-</mml:mo></mml:math></inline-formula>0.029 in 2023, confirming a significant intensification of the moisture signal (Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F8"/>b). This hydrological stress led to strong NIR absorption by the overlying water, which severely dampened the diagnostic spectral signatures of the vegetation and reduced the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR). Consequently, the extracted phenological profiles underwent major distortions that resulted in localized omission errors.</p>
      <p id="d2e4613">Second, the interplay between atmospheric constraints and observation gaps. The persistent storm systems associated with these typhoons triggered a surge in regional cloudiness. Mean cloud cover during the two critical phases reached 50.76 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M143" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and 41.25 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M144" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> respectively (Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F8"/>a). Although we maintained a 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M145" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> cloud-cover threshold for scene selection, the sheer frequency of cloudy days in 2023 drastically curtailed the availability of high-quality “clear-sky” pixels during the peak phenological stages. This scarcity forced a reliance on temporally offset or sub-optimal observations, which hindered the capacity of the model to accurately reconstruct fine-scale phenological trajectories <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx10" id="paren.76"/>. As a result, the distinctive spectral contrast of coastal vegetation was partially compromised by increased atmospheric noise and reduced observation frequency.</p>
      <p id="d2e4645">The CCAV-10m dataset possesses inherent limitations during climatically anomalous years, where the “observable area” may deviate from the “actual distribution” due to environmental noise. Future iterations of the CCAV-10m framework will prioritize the integration of SAR-derived inundation masks and multi-source high-frequency data fusion to effectively decouple ecological evolutionary signals from short-term environmental noise within complex coastal ecotones.</p>
</sec>

<sec id="Ch1.S6">
  <label>6</label><title>Data availability</title>
      <p id="d2e4657">The Sentinel-1 synthetic aperture radar (SAR) and Sentinel-2 multispectral data used in this study are available from the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (<uri>https://dataspace.copernicus.eu</uri>, last access: 21 April 2026) under the Copernicus open data policy. The species-level coastal wetland vegetation dataset, CCAV-10m, produced in this study is publicly available at the Science Data Bank (<ext-link xlink:href="https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.31077" ext-link-type="DOI">10.57760/sciencedb.31077</ext-link>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bib1.bibx33" id="altparen.77"/>). To ensure long-term spatiotemporal monitoring, we are committed to updating this dataset annually; these updates will be released as distinct versions corresponding to different year intervals (e.g. 2024, 2025, and beyond) and will be accompanied by updated technical documentation on the same platform. Validation samples comprising 84 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M146" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> of the total dataset are provided in the file “Vegetation samples.xlsx” within the same repository; these samples cover all vegetation classes and major coastal regions and were derived from field surveys, visual interpretation, and public reference sources. The remaining 16 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M147" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> of the field sampling data are not publicly released but can be obtained upon a reasonable scientific request to the authors.</p>

<sec id="Ch1.S7" sec-type="conclusions">
  <label>7</label><title>Conclusions</title>
      <p id="d2e4693">This study developed a phenology-guided coastal wetland vegetation classification network integrating Sentinel-1/2 (P_SVCN) and generated the CCAV-10m dataset, which maps the wetland vegetation types in eastern coastal China from 2016 to 2023. By integrating multi-source Sentinel-1 SAR and Sentinel-2 MSI data, the P_SVCN fully exploits structural and phenological features, enabling accurate discrimination of spectrally similar and spatially fragmented vegetation types. The results are summarized as follows: <list list-type="order"><list-item>
      <p id="d2e4698">A long-term, high-resolution species-level vegetation dataset (CCAV-10m). The dataset provides annual species-level maps of coastal wetland vegetation at a spatial resolution of 10 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M148" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, distinguishing six dominant plant types. Spatiotemporal analysis shows that <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp. is the predominant vegetation type, followed by <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, whose coverage nearly matches the combined extent of <italic>P. australis</italic>, mangroves, <italic>S. mariqueter</italic>, and <italic>T. chinensis</italic>. Further temporal assessments reveal pronounced community succession in recent years, including the continued expansion of <italic>S. alterniflora</italic> and <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., fluctuating patterns in <italic>P. australis</italic>, and a steady increase in mangrove extent. Transition matrices indicate that conversions among <italic>S. alterniflora</italic>, <italic>Suaeda</italic> spp., and <italic>P. australis</italic> occurs most frequently, reflecting the dynamic responses of coastal wetland ecosystems to natural processes and human activities.</p></list-item><list-item>
      <p id="d2e4745">A novel phenology-guided deep-learning framework (P_SVCN). The P_SVCN model exhibits strong classification performance, achieving an overall accuracy of 0.916 and a Kappa coefficient of 0.898 based on validation with 4668 in situ samples. These results exceed those of the baseline SVCN model (overall accuracy 0.874, Kappa 0.845). Ablation experiments further demonstrate the complementary strengths of Sentinel-1 synthetic aperture radar and Sentinel-2 optical observations, particularly in distinguishing vegetation types with highly similar spectral properties or fragmented spatial patterns.</p></list-item></list></p>
      <p id="d2e4748">CCAV-10m effectively bridges the gap between coarse-resolution ecosystem maps and single-species products by balancing species-level classification accuracy and large-scale spatial coverage. The dataset provides high-precision spatiotemporal information for monitoring vegetation succession, assessing invasive species, estimating blue carbon stocks, and supporting ecological restoration planning. Moreover, it offers a robust foundation for sustainable coastal ecosystem management under global environmental change. The CCAV-10m dataset is publicly available at  <ext-link xlink:href="https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.31077" ext-link-type="DOI">10.57760/sciencedb.31077</ext-link>, supporting reproducibility and further applications in related research.</p>
</sec>

<app id="App1.Ch1.S2">
  <label>Appendix B</label><title>Quality Control Procedures for Vegetation Samples</title>
<sec id="App1.Ch1.S2.SS1">
  <label>B1</label><title>Validation of Interpretation Reliability via Field-Truth Benchmarking</title>
      <p id="d2e5865">To ensure the high quality and representativeness of the training data, we implemented a rigorous validation process for our visual interpretation criteria. All interpretation was performed using high-resolution Google Earth imagery, which provides the sub-meter level detail necessary to distinguish coastal wetland vegetation.</p>
      <p id="d2e5868">To quantify the reliability of our interpretation criteria, we conducted a consistency verification specifically at the locations of the field-surveyed sites. In this process, interpreters were required to perform a blind identification of the vegetation types by examining the high-resolution Google Earth imagery corresponding to each field-surveyed coordinate. By cross-referencing these image-based interpretation results with the actual ground-truth observations, we calculated the Kappa coefficient (<inline-formula><mml:math id="M153" display="inline"><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">κ</mml:mi></mml:math></inline-formula>) using Eq. (<xref ref-type="disp-formula" rid="Ch1.E10"/>).</p>
      <p id="d2e5880">Our verification yielded a Kappa value of 0.95, confirming that the classification features observed on Google Earth imagery highly match the actual vegetation types on the ground.</p>
</sec>
<sec id="App1.Ch1.S2.SS2">
  <label>B2</label><title>Inter-Interpreter Cross-Validation</title>
      <p id="d2e5891">To further mitigate subjective bias, a double-blind review process was implemented. Each sample point was independently reviewed by at least two experienced researchers. Only samples where both researchers reached a 100 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M154" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> consensus were retained in the final dataset. This rigorous cross-verification ensured the internal consistency and reliability of the sample pool.</p>
      <p id="d2e5902">Following the quality control and cross-verification processes, the field-surveyed data and the validated interpreted samples were integrated into a unified high-confidence dataset. To further refine the sample pool, we excluded points located in mixed pixels or transition zones to prevent spectral contamination. Finally, this refined dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (70 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M155" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) and a validation set (30 <inline-formula><mml:math id="M156" display="inline"><mml:mrow class="unit"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">%</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>). This partitioning strategy ensures that the model is trained on reliable data while maintaining an independent set for unbiased accuracy assessment.</p>
</sec>
